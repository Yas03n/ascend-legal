How much protein do you actually need? A beginner’s guide

There are two public numbers worth knowing: a long-standing minimum for healthy adults, and a higher protein goal in the current Dietary Guidelines. Neither one is a personal prescription. Here is how to turn them into a plate you can actually eat.

Start with the official floor

The Food and Nutrition Board’s Dietary Reference Intakes set the adult protein Recommended Dietary Allowance at 0.8 grams per kilogram of body weight per day. On the published table, that works out to 56 grams a day for the reference man and 46 grams a day for the reference woman. The footnote on the National Academies DRI table says the adult figure is based on 0.8 g/kg for the reference body weight. An RDA is defined there as the average daily intake sufficient to meet the needs of nearly all healthy people in a group, about 97 to 98 percent.

In kitchen terms: take your weight in pounds, divide by 2.2 to get kilograms, then multiply by 0.8.

  • 154 pounds is about 70 kilograms. 70 × 0.8 = 56 grams of protein a day.
  • 125 pounds is about 57 kilograms. 57 × 0.8 = about 46 grams a day.
  • 180 pounds is about 82 kilograms. 82 × 0.8 = about 66 grams a day.

That number is a floor for avoiding a shortfall in healthy adults. It is not a score you must hit at one meal, and it is not automatically the right target if you are growing, pregnant, ill, or training with a specific goal. If a clinician has given you a protein limit, for example because of kidney disease, their number wins. This page cannot see your chart.

The newer guideline is higher

The Dietary Guidelines for Americans, 2025–2030, from the U.S. Department of Health and Human Services and the U.S. Department of Agriculture, tell people to prioritize protein foods at meals. The protein serving goal in that edition is 1.2 to 1.6 grams per kilogram of body weight per day, adjusted for individual calorie needs. The same section says to eat a variety of protein foods from animal sources, such as eggs, poultry, seafood, and meat, and from plant sources, such as beans, peas, lentils, nuts, seeds, and soy. The PDF is on realfood.gov.

Using the same 70 kilogram example:

  • 1.2 g/kg × 70 kg = 84 grams a day.
  • 1.6 g/kg × 70 kg = 112 grams a day.

So a healthy adult around 154 pounds might see a public range that runs from the RDA of 56 grams up through a guideline goal around 84 to 112 grams, depending on calorie needs. That is a wide band on purpose. Smaller people land lower. Larger people land higher. Someone eating very few calories may struggle to reach the top of the band without crowding out other food, which is why the guidelines say to adjust for calorie needs.

Do not treat the top of that band as a dare. More protein is not automatically better, and piling on protein while ignoring the rest of the plate is how people end up with a stack of bars and no vegetables. The useful move is to pick a number in the published range that you can reach with normal meals, then look at what you actually eat for a week.

What protein is doing, in plain words

Protein is one of the three energy-containing nutrients, along with carbohydrates and fat. Your body uses the amino acids from protein foods to build and repair tissue, including muscle. The Dietary Guidelines treat protein foods as a core part of the pattern, not as a supplement aisle. You still need carbohydrates for a lot of daily movement, and you still need fats. A protein tracker is helpful because it shows the split, not because the other two stopped mattering.

If you just started the kind of training described in how to start working out, you do not need to overhaul dinner on day one. You need enough protein that the work has something to build from, and enough total food that you are not miserable. Strength work plus a very low-calorie diet is a hard combination for a beginner to read. Energy crashes get blamed on the workouts. Sometimes the workouts were fine and lunch was a coffee.

A practical way to spread it

The guidelines say to prioritize protein foods at every meal. A practical reading, not a lab rule: put a clear protein food on the plate at breakfast, lunch, and dinner, instead of saving the entire target for a giant dinner. People who “eat clean” all day and then discover they are at 20 grams of protein at 7 p.m. usually did not have a willpower problem. They had a breakfast of toast and a lunch of chips.

You do not need a gram scale to start. You need the habit of noticing. Packaged foods list protein on the label. Chain restaurants often post it, which is what the restaurant guides on this site quote. A palm-sized portion of meat, fish, or tofu is a rough visual, not a measurement. If you want the real number, weigh it a few times until your eye calibrates, or log the label.

Breakfast ideas that are just food

  • Eggs and fruit, or eggs and toast. Read the carton for the protein per egg and multiply.
  • Plain yogurt or cottage cheese with fruit. Use the tub’s label, because brands differ.
  • Leftover chicken or beans if you like savory breakfasts. There is no rule that protein has to be sweet in the morning.

Lunch and dinner

Build the plate in this order: a protein food, a vegetable or fruit, a carbohydrate you like, and a fat that makes the meal satisfying. Then check the protein once, not every bite. If you eat out, start from a published number instead of guessing. The guides for Chipotle, Chick-fil-A, and In-N-Out use each chain’s own nutrition info and show the date.

Shakes, bars, and ordinary meals

A shake or a bar can fill a gap when you are between meetings. It is not more “serious” than a piece of chicken and a potato. Read the label the same way you would read any other package: protein, calories, and the serving size, which is sometimes half the package. If the only protein you eat comes from sweetened bars, you will hit a number and still feel like you did not eat. Use them as a backup.

Plant proteins count. Beans, lentils, tofu, tempeh, dairy if you use it, eggs, and soy foods are all protein foods in the guidelines. You may need a larger portion of beans than of chicken to reach the same grams. That is arithmetic, not a verdict on the meal. Combine them in a way you will actually repeat. A burrito bowl with chicken and beans is a common way people land a high number without a second dinner. The Chipotle guide shows the published grams for those ingredients so you can add them yourself.

What not to do with the number

  • Do not drop carbohydrates to “make room” if you are also trying to walk and lift. The guidelines still include grains, fruit, and vegetables. Protein is a priority, not the whole plate.
  • Do not copy a gram target from a stranger’s social post. Their weight, their sport, and their clinician are not yours.
  • Do not skip meals to bank protein for later. By evening you are hungry, and the plan becomes whatever is closest.
  • Do not treat one high-protein restaurant order as the whole day. Log it, then eat the other meals like a person.

Questions

How much protein does a beginner need?

The long-standing adult Recommended Dietary Allowance is 0.8 grams of protein per kilogram of body weight per day. The Dietary Guidelines for Americans, 2025–2030, also give a protein serving goal of 1.2 to 1.6 grams per kilogram per day, adjusted for calorie needs. Use those as public reference points, then get personal advice if you have a medical condition.

Is the RDA the same as a target for someone who lifts?

No. The RDA is the intake estimated to cover nearly all healthy adults and prevent deficiency. It was not written as a bodybuilding target. The newer Dietary Guidelines goal is higher. People who train hard, are pregnant, or are healing sometimes need a different number. A clinician or dietitian should set that, not a website.

Do I have to drink protein shakes?

No. Shakes are just food in a cup. Ordinary meals can cover protein. A shake is a convenience when you are short on time, not a requirement for getting started.

How can I tell what I actually eat?

Read the label on packaged food, use the nutrition info restaurants publish, and log it. Ascend is a protein tracker as well as a workout app: protein, carbs, fat, and calories update as you log, including from a photo or a barcode.

This is general information, not medical advice. Talk with a doctor or a registered dietitian before you change how you train or eat, especially if you have a health condition, are pregnant, or have been told to limit protein.
